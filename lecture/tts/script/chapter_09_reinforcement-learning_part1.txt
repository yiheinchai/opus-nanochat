Okay, and now the last stage, and I think the most exciting one. Why do we need it? Karpathy has a great analogy. Think of a textbook. Most of it is exposition, background knowledge. That's pre-training. Then there are worked examples, where an expert solves a problem step by step, and you imitate them. That's S F T. And at the end of every chapter, there are practice problems, with the final answer in the back of the book but no solution. You try, you fail, you try again, and you figure out what works for you. That's reinforcement learning.

And here's the deep reason why this matters. With S F T, we make the model imitate a solution that some person, or some other model, wrote. But that author has a very different brain. A step that's trivial for them might be a leap that our little model can't make in one token. And a step they wrote out carefully might be obvious to our model. We don't actually know the best sequence of tokens for this particular model. So the idea of RL is: don't tell it. Let it try many times, and whatever works, do more of that.

So let's make that precise. We have a question, the model samples an answer y, and we check it with our grader and get a reward R, one if it's correct and zero if not. And we want to maximize the expected reward, the average reward of the answers the model would sample. Now how do we get a gradient of an average over samples? Here's the trick, and it's worth deriving once. The expected reward is a sum over all possible answers of the probability of the answer times its reward, so its gradient is the sum of the reward times the gradient of the probability. Now the gradient of p is the same thing as p times the gradient of log p, that's just the chain rule backwards. And plugging that in, we get an expectation again! The average, over sampled answers, of the reward times the gradient of the log probability. And an expectation we can estimate by sampling. And the log probability of an answer is the sum of the log probabilities of its tokens, which is just minus the loss our model already computes.

So in code, it's almost embarrassingly simple. Sample some answers, and for each one, take the log probability of its tokens, multiply by its reward, and do gradient ascent on that. If you squint, it's just S F T on your own samples, weighted by how good they were. This is called the policy gradient, or reinforce.
